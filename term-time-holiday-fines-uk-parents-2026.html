TERM TIME HOLIDAY FINES: WHAT UK PARENTS ACTUALLY NEED TO KNOW

More than a third of UK parents say they're planning to take their children out of school for a holiday this year, and plenty more have already done it. Fines for it are real, they've topped £41 million across England and Wales since 2022, and the rules changed in 2024 in a way a lot of people haven't caught up with yet. Here's exactly what the current rules actually say, what it genuinely costs if you get it wrong, and how to think about the decision properly rather than going on what you've heard at the school gate.

The short version

In England, a national framework that came into force on 19 August 2024 sets one standard fine structure for every school: £80 per parent per child if you pay within 21 days, rising to £160 if it drags on, and a flat £160 with no discount if it happens again within 3 years. A third time within 3 years skips the fine altogether and goes straight to court. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland all handle it differently, and none of them use England's figures.

None of this makes a family holiday in term time illegal exactly, it means it's unauthorised absence unless your headteacher agrees it's an exceptional circumstance, and unauthorised absence is what the fine is actually for.

England's actual rules, since the 2024 change

The Department for Education's National Framework for Penalty Notices applies to every state school in England from 19 August 2024 onwards, replacing the old system where each local authority set its own figures. It's a single, consistent set of fines, and it escalates quickly if it happens more than once.

Occurrence (within a rolling 3 years)If paid within 21 daysIf paid within 22 to 28 days
First offence£80 per parent, per child£160 per parent, per child
Second offenceNo reduced rate, flat £160 per parent, per child£160 per parent, per child
Third offence or moreNo penalty notice issued. Referred straight to the Magistrates' Court, with fines of up to £2,500 and a possible criminal record that can later appear on a parent's DBS check.

Both parents can be fined separately for the same absence, so for two parents and two children that's up to £320 at the first offence rate, or £640 if it happens a second time, well before anyone goes near court.

What actually triggers a penalty notice

The national threshold is 10 sessions of unauthorised absence within a rolling 10 school week period. A school day counts as 2 sessions, so that's the equivalent of 5 full days, but they don't need to be 5 days in a row. A long weekend added to half term, plus an unrelated day off a few weeks later, can add up to the same threshold even though neither looked like much on its own, and the rolling window can span two different terms or even two school years.

This is a national minimum, not a maximum. A school or local authority can still choose to issue a penalty notice for unauthorised absence below that threshold if it wants to, the 10 session rule just guarantees it as the baseline everywhere.

Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland do this differently

England's national framework only applies in England. The other three nations don't use the same figures, and it's worth knowing that before assuming the rules above apply everywhere in the UK.

NationHow it actually works
WalesEach local council sets its own penalty notice code of conduct rather than following England's national figures, so the exact amount varies by area. Conwy's current code, for example, charges £60 if paid within 28 days, rising to £120 if paid within 42 days, capped at one penalty notice per parent per term. Check your own council's code rather than assuming it matches England or another Welsh council.
ScotlandNo fixed penalty notice system equivalent to England's. Scottish Government guidance treats a term time family holiday as unauthorised absence unless the circumstances are genuinely exceptional, and it's the local council, not a set fine scale, that decides what happens next. Contact your council's education department directly rather than assuming either way.
Northern IrelandAlso doesn't run a fixed fine scale like England's. If attendance becomes a concern, the Education Authority's Education Welfare Service can get involved and the response is handled case by case rather than through a set penalty amount, so check directly with your child's school or the Education Authority before you book.

Is it actually worth it? What the numbers say

A Confused.com survey of 1,000 UK parents in January 2026 found 34% are planning to take their children out of school for a holiday this year, and 40% said they're unfazed by warnings, fines or legal action. Parents who've done it before reported average savings of £605 per holiday, and 37% said outright that they think a fine is simply cheaper than school holiday prices.

The enforcement side is real too. The same research found more than £41 million has been paid in fines across England and Wales, with over 873,000 fixed penalty notices issued between 2022 and 2024 based on Freedom of Information requests. Essex County Council issued more than any other authority, over 35,605 since 2022, and 32% of parents surveyed said they personally have received a fine or warning.

Run your own numbers before assuming the fine is automatically the cheaper option. A £605 average saving against a first offence fine of £80 to £160 per parent per child can genuinely still come out ahead for some families, but that gap closes fast on a second occasion, or with more than two children, or if it tips into the no-fine, straight to court category.

How to actually handle it properly

  • 1Ask the headteacher directly and early, in writing. Authorisation for term time leave is always at the headteacher's discretion and only for genuinely exceptional circumstances, never an automatic right, so a normal family holiday is unlikely to be granted. It's still always worth asking properly rather than assuming either way or just not showing up.
  • 2If it's refused, weigh the real risk honestly. Work out the actual fine for your own family size against the actual saving for your own trip, rather than going on "fines are cheaper" as a blanket rule. With two parents and two children, a second occurrence alone can reach £640 before court is even on the table.
  • 3Consider the edges of a normal school holiday instead of the middle of term. Travelling right at the start or the very end of an existing half term or holiday is still authorised time, carries no fine risk at all, and is often meaningfully cheaper than the peak week in the middle, even if it's not quite as cheap as a genuine term time trip.
  • 4Keep the rest of your child's attendance strong. Schools and local authorities generally look at the wider pattern, not just one trip in isolation, so an otherwise excellent attendance record is viewed differently to a pattern of repeated unauthorised absence.
  • 5If you are fined, pay it promptly. It roughly doubles if left unpaid past the initial window, and an unpaid fine can be referred for prosecution rather than simply written off.

£80 per parent per child if paid within 21 days, rising to £160 if paid within 22 to 28 days, for a first offence. A second offence within 3 years is a flat £160 per parent per child with no reduced rate. A third offence within 3 years means no penalty notice at all, the case goes straight to the Magistrates' Court instead, with fines of up to £2,500 possible.

How many days off school actually trigger a fine?
