ANTARCTICA

Ice a hundred metres high, penguins that have never learned to be afraid of people, and a silence you genuinely cannot find anywhere else on earth. Antarctica is the one trip almost every well travelled client of mine still has on their list, and it is booked differently to anywhere else I sell. Here is exactly how I put together an expedition cruise to the White Continent, the real voyages I can price up for you, and what it actually costs.

What Jake says about Antarctica

Antarctica is not a flight and hotel holiday, it is an expedition cruise, and that changes almost everything about how it is booked. There is no resort to pick, no city to wander, you are on a small ship with an expedition team, and the whole trip is built around getting you ashore by inflatable Zodiac boat onto the ice itself, surrounded by penguins, seals and, if you are lucky, whales feeding right beside the boat.

I compare the real voyages on sale across the expedition cruise lines I work with to find the ship, route and cabin that actually fits your budget and dates, since every single one of these trips is sold as its own named departure rather than a generic package.

!
Jake's top tip

The one thing I always ask a new Antarctica client is how they feel about the Drake Passage crossing. Some people love two sea days watching for albatross, others would rather fly over it entirely. Both are genuinely available, and it is the first thing that decides which voyage suits you.

Trip length & who it suits

The shortest fly cruise options run to around 6 days, 5 nights, but most clients book 10 to 13 nights for the classic round trip from Ushuaia, or up to 19 or 20 nights for a voyage that also takes in South Georgia and the Falkland Islands. Add the flights and a night or two either side in Buenos Aires or Ushuaia itself, and the whole trip away from home usually runs to around two to three weeks.

It suits clients who want a genuine once in a lifetime trip built around wildlife and scenery rather than relaxation, anyone comfortable on a small ship, and travellers happy with cold weather, early starts for landings and a day or two at sea getting there. It is less suited to anyone with very limited mobility, since getting in and out of a Zodiac onto rocky or icy shorelines takes a reasonable level of fitness, or to clients on a tight budget, since this is one of the most expensive trips I book. I am always upfront about that before anyone gets too attached to a particular voyage.

Getting there

There are no direct flights from the UK to the southern tip of South America. Most voyages route clients via an overnight flight to Buenos Aires, Argentina, then on to Ushuaia, the world's southernmost city and the usual departure port, for a total journey time of around 17 to 20 hours door to door before the ship even leaves harbour. A smaller number of voyages instead fly you to Punta Arenas in Chile for a fly cruise that skips the sea crossing entirely.

From Ushuaia, reaching the Antarctic Peninsula itself means crossing the Drake Passage, around two days of open ocean between the southern tip of South America and the South Shetland Islands. It can be perfectly calm, known among crew as the Drake Lake, or genuinely rough, known as the Drake Shake, and nobody can promise you which one you will get. The alternative is a fly cruise, flying directly from Punta Arenas to the South Shetland Islands in around two hours and joining a smaller expedition ship once you land, which avoids the crossing completely but costs more and depends on a landable airstrip, so flights can occasionally be delayed by weather.

!
Jake's top tip

I always build a buffer night in Buenos Aires or Ushuaia into the itinerary either side of the cruise, never a tight same day connection, since a missed flight this far south is a genuinely expensive problem to fix.

Weather by month

Antarctica only has a tourist season at all between roughly November and March, the southern hemisphere summer, when the sea ice breaks up enough for ships to reach the Peninsula. These are typical conditions for the Antarctic Peninsula itself across that season.

MonthAvg temperatureWhat to expect
Novemberaround -2°CSeason opens, colder and less settled, penguins arriving and pairing up to nest, pristine untouched snow
Decemberaround -1°CClose to 24 hours of daylight, penguin chicks hatching, still a lot of fresh snow underfoot
Januaryaround 0°C, occasionally up to 15°C in sheltered spotsHeight of the season, generally the most settled weather and over 20 hours of daylight, penguin chicks active everywhere
Februaryaround 1°CWarmer seas bring more whales, often the best month for whale watching, and the best chance of reaching further south towards the Polar Circle
Marchcooling quickly, back towards 0°C and belowSeason closing, fewer ships still sailing, shorter days, but excellent whale sightings as a last hurrah before winter

Approximate seasonal averages for the Antarctic Peninsula, compiled from expedition operator climate guides. No two seasons behave exactly the same, and the captain always has the final say on where a ship can actually go.

!
Jake's top tip

Pack like you are going somewhere properly cold even in January. Most voyages supply a polar jacket and boots, but thermal layers, a warm hat and good gloves are down to you, and the wind off the ice makes it feel far colder than the number on the thermometer.

Best time to visit

November suits clients who want the most pristine, untouched scenery and to see penguins courting and nesting, though it is colder and some landing sites are still snowed in. December and January are the classic months, with the longest days and penguin chicks everywhere, and January in particular tends to bring the calmest, most settled conditions. February and March are my pick for whale watching, as the water warms enough to draw in more krill and the whales that feed on it, and February also gives ships the best shot at pushing further south towards the Polar Circle. Whichever month, availability on the more popular departures books up a long way ahead, sometimes a year or more, so this is not a trip to leave to the last minute.

Three ways to see Antarctica

Antarctica does not come as one simple flight and hotel package, every trip is its own named expedition voyage, so the real choice is which style of voyage suits you. Here are three genuine, bookable options to show how different they can be.

Classic round trip from Ushuaia

13 nights door to door, around 10 nights on board, on the MS Fridtjof Nansen with HX Expeditions. Flies overnight from London to Buenos Aires, a hotel night there, then on to Ushuaia to join the ship for the Drake Passage crossing both ways and around five days exploring the Peninsula itself. Full board, drinks, a polar jacket and all landings included. From £10,299pp.

Fly cruise, no Drake Passage

11 nights with Antarctica21, flying from Santiago to Punta Arenas, then a two hour charter flight straight to the South Shetland Islands to join a small expedition ship, the Magellan Explorer, for days of landings and Zodiac cruising. The one to pick if seasickness is a genuine worry. From £14,485pp, including flights, transfers and most meals and drinks on board.

Antarctica, South Georgia & the Falklands

19 nights with AE Expeditions on the Sylvia Earle, departing Ushuaia and finishing in Punta Arenas. Takes in the Falkland Islands, six days around South Georgia's enormous king penguin colonies, then four days on the Antarctic Peninsula itself. The full expedition for anyone with the time and the budget. From £27,329pp.

Prices shown are the lowest advertised per person fare found for each voyage at the time of writing, based on two sharing, and are subject to change, availability and exchange rates. I always check the live price and cabin availability before quoting.

Where to stay

There is no hotel to choose, the ship is where you stay for the whole trip, so this is really a choice of cabin. The MS Fridtjof Nansen carries up to around 500 guests across outside cabins, balcony cabins and a range of suites, with an infinity pool, hot tubs, a science centre and a proper restaurant on board. The Sylvia Earle is a smaller, 70 cabin ship with an Ulstein X Bow designed to handle rough seas more comfortably, a two storey atrium lounge and a wellness centre with a sauna. Antarctica21's Magellan Explorer is smaller again, a genuinely boutique ship built specifically for the fly cruise route. Across all three, every cabin is ensuite and every voyage includes a proper polar jacket and boots to keep, which is worth factoring in against what you would otherwise spend on kit.

!
Jake's top tip

Cabins on every Antarctica ship sell out from the lowest category upwards, so if you have a particular voyage in mind, book it as early as you can rather than waiting to see the cheapest cabins disappear first.

Getting around

Every landing and excursion is done by inflatable Zodiac boat, launched straight from the ship, with the expedition team driving and briefing you before every single outing. There is nowhere to drive yourself and nothing to book separately once you are on board, landings and activities are built into the voyage itself and simply announced each day depending on the weather, ice and wildlife. Most voyages also offer optional add ons such as kayaking, camping out on the ice for a night, or a polar plunge into freezing water, usually bookable in advance or once on board, and these are the only extras most clients choose to add.

Places to visit

Whale watching in the Gerlache Strait

Humpback and minke whales feed in huge numbers through the Peninsula's channels from February onwards, often surfacing right alongside the Zodiacs. The expedition team will hold position quietly for as long as the whales stay interested, which can be a very long time.

Penguin colonies on the Peninsula

Gentoo, chinstrap and Adelie penguins nest in their thousands on Antarctic Peninsula shorelines such as Danco Island and Cuverville Island, close enough on a landing to watch chicks being fed without ever approaching them yourself, which the expedition team's guidelines are strict about.

Seals hauled out on the ice

Weddell, crabeater and leopard seals are a regular sight hauled out on ice floes during Zodiac cruises, usually too relaxed to bother moving as the boats pass, though the occasional curious leopard seal will swim right up alongside for a closer look.

Icebergs & Paradise Harbour

Enormous tabular icebergs calved from glaciers drift through the Peninsula's channels in every shade of white and blue, and Paradise Harbour in particular is a favourite stop for Zodiac cruising among them and, ice permitting, a landing on the continent itself.

Quick practical info

The essentials, at a glance.

CurrencyNo currency is used in Antarctica itself. Argentine Peso or Chilean Peso at the gateway port, depending on your route, cards widely accepted in Buenos Aires and Santiago
Plug typeType C and I in Argentina, Type C and L in Chile, voltage 220V, a UK adaptor is needed. Cabins on most ships have UK and US style sockets fitted
LanguageSpanish at the gateway ports, English is the working language on board every expedition ship
Flight time from the UKNo direct flights. Around 17 to 20 hours total via Buenos Aires or Santiago, before the ship departs
Time difference3 hours behind the UK in winter, 4 hours behind during British Summer Time
Entry requirementsUK passport holders need no visa for Argentina or Chile, and Antarctica itself requires no visa since it is not a sovereign nation. Your passport should be valid for at least 6 months beyond your arrival date
InsuranceEvery expedition cruise line requires specialist travel insurance that explicitly covers polar regions and emergency evacuation, standard annual policies are not usually enough. I always check this is sorted before final payment
