UK PACKAGE HOLIDAY RULES ARE CHANGING IN APRIL 2027: WHAT IT ACTUALLY MEANS FOR HOW YOU BOOK

A few clients have asked me lately whether anything's changing with how package holidays are protected, usually after seeing a headline about new travel rules somewhere. There is something genuinely changing, it's just not landing for a while yet. New regulations have been made that widen what legally counts as a protected package holiday, and get rid of a weaker halfway category called a linked travel arrangement altogether. Here's exactly what's changing, when it actually starts, and what it means for a holiday you book now versus one you book after the new rules land.

Three things worth knowing before it affects you

This is a genuine legal change, not a rumour, but it isn't about to catch anyone out overnight.

The date that matters

Nothing changes for a booking you make now

The new rules only apply to package contracts entered into on or after 6 April 2027. Anything booked before that date stays under the current 2018 rules, whenever your holiday actually takes place.

The definition itself

What counts as a "package" is getting wider

More combinations of travel services bought separately, but in one visit to one seller, will qualify for full package protection once the new rules are in force.

What's disappearing

Linked travel arrangements are being scrapped

This weaker, lighter touch protection category is removed from the law entirely. Anything that doesn't meet the new, wider package definition loses this protection rather than falling back on it.

What's actually changing, in plain English

The instrument itself is called the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements (Amendment) Regulations 2026, registered as UK Statutory Instrument 2026 No. 455, and it amends the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018, the law that currently governs package holiday protection in this country. It was debated in Parliament during April 2026 and formally made shortly after. You can read the actual legal text on legislation.gov.uk if you want to see it in full.

The important detail most coverage of this has buried is the start date. These changes come into force on 6 April 2027, and they are not retrospective. The regulations are explicit that they only apply to package contracts, or what would previously have been linked travel arrangements, entered into on or after that date. A holiday you book this month, or any time before 6 April 2027, is unaffected, even if you're travelling well after that date.

Jake's top tip

If you're booking a holiday for travel after April 2027, it's the date you book that matters for which rules apply, not the date you fly. Book in March 2027 and you're under the current rules for that whole trip.

The wider "package" definition explained

Right now, a package generally means pre arranged combinations of at least two different travel services, like a flight and a hotel, sold together for one price. Under the new rules, separately contracted and separately paid for travel services can also count as a full package if a single trader facilitated you selecting and paying for each one separately during one visit to, or one contact with, its point of sale.

In practice, that's aimed at situations like an airline offering you a hotel or car hire as a follow on purchase straight after booking your flight, a booking platform with separate tabs or steps for flights and accommodation that you move through in one sitting, or a hotel's own website selling you a local excursion as a separate add on during the same booking visit. Under the current rules, arrangements like these have often fallen into a weaker category or sometimes no package protection at all. Under the new rules, more of them will qualify as full packages, which brings full pre contractual information rights, organiser liability if something goes wrong, and insolvency protection.

!
Jake's top tip

If you're ever unsure whether something you're booking in stages counts as a package, ask the seller directly before you pay for the final piece. If they can't give you a straight answer, that's worth treating as a red flag in itself.

Linked travel arrangements are disappearing

A linked travel arrangement, or LTA, is the current law's halfway category. It covers certain combinations of separately bought travel services that don't qualify as a full package, but still get some protection, mainly insolvency protection and a set of information requirements, just less of it than a package gets. From 6 April 2027, this category is removed from the law completely. The relevant repeal covers the regulation that set out LTA insolvency protection and information duties, along with the schedules that spelled out exactly what businesses had to tell you under it.

What that means practically is that anything which would previously have been an LTA goes one of two ways once the new rules apply. If it now meets the new, wider package definition, it moves up into full package protection, which is better than what LTA status gave it. If it doesn't meet that wider definition, it loses LTA protection altogether and isn't automatically covered by anything in its place. That's the genuine catch in an otherwise consumer friendly sounding change, and it's worth not assuming you're covered just because an older guide mentions linked travel arrangements.

!
Jake's top tip

Once these rules are in force, I wouldn't rely on the term linked travel arrangement meaning anything protective any more. If a seller uses it to explain your booking after April 2027, ask what protection you actually have in its place.

Faster refunds and a new right of redress behind the scenes

A couple of the other changes matter more to how travel businesses operate behind the scenes than to what you see directly, but they're worth knowing about because they affect how reliably your money moves if something is cancelled. A new rule requires a third party supplier, say an airline or a hotel, that cancels or fails to provide a travel service, to refund the organiser or retailer who booked it within 14 days of the cancellation or the date the service was due, whichever comes first. A separate new rule gives organisers and retailers a right to claim damages from suppliers that caused the problem in the first place.

Neither of these pays you directly, they operate between the businesses in the chain, but they matter because an organiser who's waiting months for a supplier to return money is exactly the kind of cash flow problem that can tip a struggling travel company into real trouble. There's also a new rule closing a loophole some businesses have used, a company can no longer get itself out of its package travel obligations simply by declaring that it's only acting as an intermediary, or that a particular booking isn't a package, if the facts say otherwise.

!
Jake's top tip

None of this is something you need to action yourself, it's really a note for anyone who likes to understand the whole picture. The part that actually affects your own booking is the wider package definition and the end of linked travel arrangements above.

What this means for how you book

  • 1Nothing changes for any booking made before 6 April 2027. Whatever you book this year or early next year stays under the current rules for the whole of that trip.
  • 2Closer to and after that date, check whether you're buying one package or several separate contracts. Ask the seller directly which applies, rather than assuming.
  • 3More flight, hotel and extras combinations bought in one sitting from one seller will get full package protection once the rules are in force. That's generally a win for the kind of booking that used to fall into a grey area.
  • 4Linked travel arrangements stop existing as a protection category. Don't assume older advice about them still applies after April 2027.
  • 5A business can no longer talk its way out of package obligations by calling itself just an intermediary. That loophole is specifically closed.

Quick answers

When do these new rules actually start?

6 April 2027. They only apply to package contracts, or arrangements that would previously have been linked travel arrangements, entered into on or after that date, not to bookings made before it.

Does this change anything about a holiday I book for summer 2027?

Only if you book it on or after 6 April 2027. If you book before that date, even for travel later in 2027, you're under the current 2018 rules for that whole booking.

What was a linked travel arrangement, and why does it matter that it's going?

It's the current law's lighter protection category for certain combinations of separately bought travel services that don't qualify as a full package. It's being removed entirely, so anything that doesn't qualify as a full package under the new, wider definition won't automatically get this fallback protection any more.

Will this make it easier or harder to get my money back if something goes wrong?

It's genuinely mixed. More combinations of travel services bought in one visit to one seller will now count as full packages, which is stronger protection. But anything that doesn't meet that wider definition loses the linked travel arrangement protection it might have had, so it's worth checking rather than assuming either way.

Does this affect ATOL protection on flight inclusive bookings?

The amending regulations also update the Civil Aviation (Air Travel Organisers' Licensing) Regulations 2012 to keep it consistent with the changes, but ATOL itself isn't being abolished or replaced. Always still check for an ATOL certificate on any flight inclusive package.
