MOST TRAVEL INSURANCE DOESN'T COVER SKIING: WHAT TO CHECK BEFORE YOU GO

Half term and the start of the main ski season are around the corner, and this is the one thing I see catch people out more than anything else with a winter sports trip. Most standard travel insurance simply doesn't cover skiing or snowboarding unless you've specifically added it, and your GHIC card won't fill the gap either. Here's exactly what proper cover needs to include, and the genuine mistakes that get claims turned down on the mountain.

Three things worth knowing before you book

A ski holiday is genuinely one of the few trip types where the insurance side deserves more than a tick box glance. There are three separate gaps that catch people out, and they're easy to mix up.

The policy gap

Winter sports cover isn't automatic

Most single trip and annual travel insurance policies don't include skiing or snowboarding as standard. It has to be added specifically.

The health card gap

A GHIC card doesn't cover mountain rescue

Your free UK Global Health Insurance Card is genuinely useful in Europe, but it was never designed to replace travel insurance on the slopes.

The claims gap

Common mistakes get claims rejected

Going off-piste without a qualified guide, or skiing after a few drinks, are two of the most common reasons winter sports claims don't pay out.

Only 6% of policies include winter sports cover as standard

Which? published research in January 2026 showing just how big this gap really is. An analysis of Defaqto data by Go.Compare, covering 923 single trip policies and 923 annual policies, found that only 6% of single trip policies and 11% of annual policies include winter sports cover as standard. Which?'s own separate analysis of 160 policies found the same thing, just 6% included it automatically. For its wider research, Which? surveyed 59 travel insurance companies on their cover levels, analysed 140 winter sports policies including their piste closure terms, and asked more than 1,000 people who'd recently been on a ski holiday to rate their provider.

In practice, that means the vast majority of people heading off skiing or snowboarding this winter need to actively check their policy, and very likely add winter sports cover separately, rather than assuming their usual travel insurance already has them covered.

!
Jake's top tip

If skiing or snowboarding is part of your plan, don't assume your existing annual travel insurance already covers it. Check the policy document for the actual words 'winter sports', and if it isn't there, call your insurer and ask what it costs to add before you book anything else.

Why your GHIC card doesn't fill the gap

The UK Global Health Insurance Card, the GHIC, which replaced the old EHIC after Brexit, is free and gives you access to state healthcare in the EU and a handful of other countries, on the same terms as a local resident. It's genuinely worth having, and a new card now lasts up to 5 years, with applications open from 9 months before your current one expires through the official NHS overseas healthcare portal. Once approved, it's posted to you within 15 working days, and a Provisional Replacement Certificate is available if it hasn't arrived in time for your trip.

What it doesn't do is cover you for a winter sports injury properly. The NHS is explicit that a GHIC "is not a replacement for travel insurance", and it specifically doesn't cover ski or mountain rescue, treatment in private medical facilities (common at ski resorts), being flown home to the UK if you can't travel as planned, or any cover at all for lost or damaged equipment, cancellation or a trip cut short.

Think of it as a free top-up, not a safety net. A GHIC can reduce or remove the cost of state treatment abroad, but the genuinely expensive parts of a ski injury, mountain rescue, a medical escort home, or treatment at a private clinic because the state hospital is further away, are exactly what it leaves out.

!
Jake's top tip

A GHIC card is well worth having and renewing before any EU trip, but I always make sure clients skiing in Europe have proper winter sports travel insurance sorted as well. One without the other leaves a genuine gap.

What proper winter sports cover should actually include

Once winter sports cover is confirmed as included, it's worth checking what it actually pays out, not just that the words are there on the policy. Which?'s research points to a handful of areas that vary enormously between providers.

  • 1Medical treatment abroad. Covers injury or illness on the slopes. Decent policies set this limit extremely high, sometimes in the millions of pounds, because serious ski injuries can be genuinely expensive to treat.
  • 2Repatriation. Pays to bring you home if you can't travel as planned, including specialist transport or a medical escort where needed.
  • 3Piste rescue. Covers being brought off the mountain, by snowmobile or helicopter if needed. This is often capped well below the medical treatment limit, and some policies only pay out once you reach hospital, so it's worth checking the actual figure.
  • 4Equipment cover. For lost, stolen or damaged skis, boards or boots, and sometimes hire costs too. Limits are usually in the hundreds to low thousands of pounds.
  • 5Cancellation and curtailment. Recovers unused costs if injury or illness forces you to cancel or cut the trip short. Limits typically range from around £500 to £20,000 depending on the policy.
  • 6Piste closures and avalanches. Some policies pay out if slopes close or avalanches cause delays, though payouts are usually capped and only apply once closures last a set number of days.
  • 7Personal liability. Covers you if you injure another skier or damage their property, which is more common than people expect on a busy piste.
!
Jake's top tip

Ask your insurer for the actual pound figure on piste rescue and medical cover, not just whether the words 'winter sports' appear on the policy. Some policies technically include it with a rescue payout capped well below what a genuine mountain rescue and hospital bill can run to.

The mistakes that get winter sports claims rejected

Having winter sports cover on the policy doesn't guarantee a claim pays out. Which?'s research flagged several genuine, common reasons insurers refuse winter sports claims, and they're all avoidable if you know about them in advance.

!
Jake's top tip

If you're planning to go off-piste at all, even just ducking off the marked run for a few minutes, check your policy covers it and whether it needs to be with a qualified guide. It's one of the most common reasons winter sports claims get turned down.

The small insurance add-on sold with your lift pass

Most French resorts, and plenty in Switzerland and Italy too, sell a small add-on insurance alongside your lift pass, often known in France as Carré Neige. It's usually a small daily supplement, roughly a few euros a day, around £2 to £3.50 based on current resort pricing, or there's often a cheaper season-long version. It's recognised directly by the local rescue services, so you're not paying for a mountain rescue upfront and claiming it back later, and some versions will refund your lift pass for days lost to injury.

It's a top-up, not a replacement. This kind of resort insurance is designed to get you off the mountain and cover the rescue itself. It isn't a substitute for proper travel insurance, it won't cover your actual hospital treatment beyond what state healthcare or your GHIC provides, repatriation home, your equipment, or a cancelled or cut short trip.

It's worth taking if it's offered, since it's inexpensive and genuinely useful for the rescue itself, but treat it as one more layer on top of proper winter sports travel insurance, never instead of it.

What this actually means before you book

None of this should put anyone off a ski holiday, it's genuinely one of the best types of trip out there. It just needs a few minutes of actually reading the policy, rather than assuming.

  • 1Check the policy document specifically says "winter sports" or "skiing and snowboarding cover". Don't assume your standard travel insurance already includes it, for most people it doesn't.
  • 2Confirm whether off-piste skiing is included, and whether it only counts when you're with a qualified guide.
  • 3Check the actual pound figures for piste rescue, medical treatment and repatriation, not just whether the words appear on the policy.
  • 4Apply for or renew your GHIC for free before you go, but treat it as a helpful top-up, never a replacement for proper travel insurance.
  • 5Take the resort's own lift pass insurance if it's offered, it's inexpensive and useful for the rescue itself, but it won't cover your hospital bill, your equipment or a cancelled trip on its own.
!
Jake's top tip

I always tell clients booking a ski holiday to sort proper winter sports insurance the same day they book, not the week before they fly. It's genuinely one of the quickest things to get right, there's just no good reason to leave it until the last minute.

Quick answers

Does my normal travel insurance cover skiing?

Not usually. Research published by Which? in January 2026 found only around 6% of single trip policies and 11% of annual policies include winter sports cover as standard. Always check the policy document, and add winter sports cover specifically if it isn't already there.

Does a GHIC card cover me if I'm injured skiing?

No. A GHIC gives you access to state healthcare in the EU on the same terms as a local resident, but it doesn't cover mountain or piste rescue, treatment at private medical facilities, repatriation to the UK, or any cover for lost equipment, cancellation or a trip cut short. It's free and worth having, but it isn't a substitute for travel insurance.

Does winter sports cover include skiing off-piste?

Not always, and it's one of the most common reasons winter sports claims get rejected. Check whether your policy covers off-piste skiing specifically, and whether it only applies when you're with a qualified guide.

Is the small insurance offered with my lift pass enough on its own?

No. Many French, Swiss and Italian resorts sell a small add-on insurance with your lift pass that helps get you off the mountain if you're hurt, but it's designed as a top-up to proper travel insurance, not a replacement for it. It won't cover your medical treatment beyond state healthcare, repatriation, or a cancelled trip.

What should I actually check before booking a ski holiday?

Confirm winter sports cover is explicitly included rather than assumed, check the pound limits for medical treatment, piste rescue and repatriation, confirm equipment and cancellation cover, and check whether off-piste needs a separate add-on or a qualified guide.
